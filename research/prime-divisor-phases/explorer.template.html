A finite experiment · exact residues, visible phases

What do four colors preserve?

Compare periodic divisor phases for every integer from 0 to 100, then compare primes with one another and with odd composites. Colors describe a phase relative to a reference number. No single color means “prime.”

Four divisor clocks

For each divisor d, the point is zd(n) = exp(2πin/d). The phase offset moves this point relative to the fixed color boundaries. An exact divisor has residue zero; a matching color alone can be less precise.

01 / RESOLUTION

Placement of the boundaries matters

At 45°, divisors 2, 3, 5 and 7 isolate zero residue in one quadrant. That is enough for 8–100. Small primes 2, 3, 5 and 7 require separate handling because they divide themselves.

Larger reference primes have more closely spaced phase points. For example, p = 97 and q = 11 can share the zero-residue color without being divisible.

02 / PARITY

The two half-units are shared

Every odd integer has n/2 ending in .5, and k = (n−1)/2 is an integer. Odd primes and odd composites both obey this identity.

The half-relative phase rearranges the residues for an odd reference. Different colors after that transformation do not, by themselves, establish a prime-specific pattern.

03 / INTERPRETATION

Keep arithmetic and colors separate

Hp(q)² = zp(q) before the display offset. This is an algebraic phase identity, not a new primality theorem.

This experiment visualizes known divisibility relationships. It does not establish a new prime formula, computational speedup, or Riemann Hypothesis result.
